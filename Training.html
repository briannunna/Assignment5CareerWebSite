Physicists and astronomers typically need a Ph.D. for jobs in research and academia. However, entry-level physicist jobs in the federal government typically require a bachelor’s degree in physics.
The first step is earning an undergraduate degree in physics, astronomy, astrophysics, or a closely related field like mathematics or engineering. During this four-year program, students take rigorous foundational courses in mechanics, quantum physics, electromagnetism, and thermodynamics. Advanced mathematics—including multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations—is heavily emphasized alongside core programming skills in languages like Python. Undergraduates should actively seek out paid summer research internships or programs to gain data analysis experience and build mentorship connections for graduate school applications.
A doctoral degree in astrophysics, physics, or a related discipline is mandatory to lead research projects, secure academic faculty positions, or work in senior government labs. Graduate programs typically take four to six years to complete and combine advanced coursework in areas like stellar evolution, cosmology, and high-performance computing with original, dissertation-based research. Most physics and astronomy Ph.D. programs in the United States provide tuition waivers and a living stipend through research or teaching assistantships, meaning students are generally paid to earn their dotorate.
Following the completion of a Ph.D., aspiring astrophysicists almost always complete one or more postdoctoral research appointments lasting one to five years. These temporary fellowships allow scientists to focus entirely on independent research, publish academic papers, and build a professional reputation within the global scientific community.
